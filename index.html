One question, one answer.

Most weather apps tell you everything except what you actually need to know: should you put on shoes and walk out the door right now, or not?

This page checks the rain forecast for the next hour at your exact location and gives you a single, honest verdict — GO OUTSIDE or STAY INSIDE. No 14-day forecast. No air quality index. No pollen count. No animated radar loop. No newsletter signup. No app to install.

If the chance of rain in the next 60 minutes is meaningful, it tells you to stay in. Otherwise, the sky is your friend and you should go.

How the verdict works

The page reads your approximate location (with your permission) and queries a free, open weather data service that aggregates forecasts from national weather agencies — the same models meteorologists use. It looks at the precipitation probability and expected rainfall amount for the hour ahead. If the probability is over 50% or measurable rain is expected, you get a STAY INSIDE verdict. Otherwise, GO OUTSIDE.

That's it. There's no algorithm trying to upsell you on premium tiers, no machine-learning model trained on your engagement metrics. Just rain, yes or no, soon.

Why this exists

Weather forecast fatigue is real. You open a weather app to answer a 10-second question and get hit with sponsored content, three ad units, an "outfit suggestion" feature, and a chart of UV index forecasts for next Tuesday. By the time you've found the rain probability, you've forgotten why you were going outside.

The problem isn't a lack of data — there's more accurate weather data freely available now than at any point in human history. The problem is that the apps showing you that data are designed to keep you scrolling. This page does the opposite. Get your answer, close the tab, walk the dog.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the next-hour rain forecast?
Short-range precipitation forecasts (under 60 minutes) are among the most accurate predictions in meteorology, especially in regions with dense radar coverage. The data here comes from national weather services (NOAA in the US, DWD in Germany, Météo-France, and others) blended together. For a quick "should I go now" check, it's solid. For planning a wedding three days out, look elsewhere.
Why does it ask for my location?
Because rain in Boston has nothing to do with rain in Brooklyn. The page asks your browser for an approximate location (you can deny it) and falls back to either an IP-based estimate or a city you type in. Your coordinates are rounded to roughly neighbourhood-level before being sent anywhere. To produce the verdict, your location goes to Open-Meteo (weather forecast), BigDataCloud (reverse geocoding to display a city name) and, only if your browser blocks geolocation, to ipapi.co (IP-based city estimate). We don't run our own servers or databases, so there's nothing we store, log, or sell on our side. Full details — including hosting via Cloudflare and ads via Google AdSense are in our Privacy Policy.
What counts as "rain" here?
A measurable forecast of precipitation in the next hour, or a probability over 50%. Drizzle counts. Snow counts (you'll get wet either way). The verdict is about whether stepping outside is a bad idea, not whether the meteorological definition of "rain" applies.
It said GO OUTSIDE and I got rained on.
It happens. Forecasts deal in probabilities, and "30% chance of rain" means it'll rain about three times out of every ten times you see that number. The flip side is that 90% of the time the page tells you to go outside, you won't get rained on. We think that's a fair trade for not having to scroll past three banner ads to find out.
Does this work everywhere in the world?
Almost. The underlying data covers the entire globe at roughly 11 km resolution, with much higher resolution (1 km) in North America and Europe. Remote areas without nearby weather stations will be less accurate, but you'll still get a reasonable answer.
Is there an app?
No. That's the point. Bookmark this page on your phone's home screen and it'll behave like an app, minus the install, the permissions, the updates, and the data harvesting.
Why are there ads if you hate cluttered weather apps?
Running a website costs money and time. Two restrained ad slots — one below the verdict, one above the footer — is the bargain we're striking. They never sit between you and your answer. The ads are served by Google AdSense and may set cookies for delivery and measurement; where consent is required, you'll see a banner and can decline. Details are in our Privacy Policy. If we ever change the ad layout to be more intrusive, this paragraph will be the first thing to go.
